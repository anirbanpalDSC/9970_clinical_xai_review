Across the seven studies meeting this review's eligibility criteria, a consistent pattern emerges that cuts across method family, publication venue, and underlying model quality: an explanation is built, and in most cases a computational property of that explanation is measured, but almost nothing is measured about whether the explanation actually helps, or could safely be trusted by, the clinician it is nominally for. Six of seven studies never involve a human at any stage of evaluating the explanation. The one exception offers an unvalidated two-question survey. Not one study reaches this review's application-grounded evaluation tier, and not one satisfies any of the four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria drawn from current FDA and EU AI Act guidance. Framed against this review's three research questions, RQ2's evaluation-rigor findings are the evidentiary core of this result, and RQ3's regulatory mapping is where that evidentiary gap becomes consequential rather than merely descriptive: it is the corpus's near-uniform absence of human-grounded and application-grounded evaluation (RQ2) that directly produces its near-uniform failure to meet any regulatory-relevant-evidence criterion (RQ3). RQ1's method-and-justification findings supply necessary scaffolding for both, showing that the methods deployed are rarely chosen for a stated, comparative reason in the first place.

## Dialogue with prior work

The gap this review documents is not a new concern in the broader XAI literature. Automation-trust research has long distinguished an operator's confidence in a system from whether that confidence actually tracks the system's real reliability, a distinction formalized as appropriate reliance and elaborated across dimensions of trust calibration well before XAI-specific evaluation frameworks existed (Lee & See, 2004; Dzindolet et al., 2003). Doshi-Velez and Kim (2017) later proposed the functionally-grounded, human-grounded, and application-grounded tiers this review adopts for RQ2, explicitly to distinguish evaluations that involve no human, a human on a simplified task, and a human on the real task; and the evaluation-approaches literature separately distinguishes objective, automated assessment from human-centred assessment as two different things a study can do (Vilone & Longo, 2021). What this review adds is not any of these distinctions in themselves but evidence that, within emergency-medicine-specific applications of post-hoc explanation methods, the human-centred, calibration-oriented half of each of these distinctions is essentially unaddressed. Every trust-related claim in this corpus falls on the plausibility side of that line: either no claim is made at all, or an explanation is asserted to be trustworthy or clinically sound without any accompanying test of whether a clinician's actual reliance on the system tracked its actual correctness. This is a narrower and more specific claim than "the XAI field over-claims interpretability" in general; it is that, in the seven studies meeting this review's EM-specific inclusion criteria, plausibility and calibration are conflated in exactly the way the broader literature has already warned against, with no exception.

A reader might reasonably ask whether this is simply what a young, methods-focused subfield looks like, and whether it is unfair to hold seven studies to a regulatory-evidence standard that most of clinical AI has not yet met either. Two responses follow from the data itself rather than from a general defense of the field. First, the corpus's single most rigorously validated study by the extraction's own quality criteria (Xie et al., 2021, scoring the maximum on all five extracted quality dimensions, validated externally on tens of thousands of admissions) is also one of the six studies with zero evaluation of its explanation's effect on any human. Methodological maturity in model validation and methodological maturity in interpretability validation are demonstrably separable in this corpus, not two faces of the same underlying rigor. Second, the claim this review makes is descriptive, not causal: the evidence gap documented here is not evidence that earlier regulatory engagement would have closed it, only that the gap exists at a moment when regulatory frameworks are beginning to ask for exactly this kind of evidence pre-deployment, not after. That regulatory clock is already running independent of whether the field considers itself ready for it.

## What this corpus is small enough, and specific enough, to say

Seven included studies, drawn from an initial pool of 650 title-and-abstract records, is not a limitation to apologize for so much as a finding in its own right: applying an emergency-medicine-specific inclusion boundary to the growing clinical-XAI literature returns very few studies with any human evaluation component at all, which is itself evidence of how early-stage this specific intersection is. What follows should be read at that scale. This review does not claim that emergency-medicine XAI as a field fails to evaluate explanations; it claims that, among the seven studies that met this review's inclusion criteria, none did so beyond a functionally-grounded or, in one case, an unvalidated human-grounded level, and specifies exactly which of four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria each one failed to meet and why.

## Recommendations

Three recommendations follow directly from patterns in the extracted data, and a fourth follows from what the data as a whole implies about when governance considerations should enter this work.

First, a reporting-standard proposal: future emergency-medicine XAI studies should report explicitly against the four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria operationalized in this review's extraction schema (clinician comprehension, trust calibration, uncertainty and failure-mode transparency, and workflow safety), in the way that existing AI-specific reporting guidelines already require explicit reporting of specific methodological elements at different stages of the clinical AI pipeline: TRIPOD+AI for prediction-model development and validation (Collins et al., 2024), CONSORT-AI for randomized trials of AI interventions (Liu et al., 2020), and DECIDE-AI specifically for early-stage clinical evaluation of AI-driven decision support systems (Vasey et al., 2022), the pipeline stage most directly analogous to what this review's included studies attempt and mostly fall short of. This review's own extraction schema, having been applied to seven heterogeneous studies without requiring modification of these four criteria, is itself a candidate starting point for such a checklist.

Second, a design recommendation grounded directly in the `Method_Interface_Isolated` extraction: only one of the seven studies (Juang et al., 2026) presents its explanation through any human-facing interface at all, and even that study does not isolate the explanation method's effect from the interface's presentation choices, since only one method and one interface configuration were tested. Future work that does reach a human-centred evaluation should build in this isolation explicitly, comparing either multiple explanation methods through an identical interface or a fixed method across interface variants, so that an observed effect on comprehension or trust can be attributed to the explanation method itself rather than confounded with how it happened to be displayed.

Third, an evaluation-tier push: given that six of seven studies in this corpus never move past a functionally-grounded, proxy-metric evaluation, the field would benefit from treating application-grounded evaluation, real clinicians completing the real target decision in a realistic setting, as a precondition for any public claim about clinical trust, decision support, or deployment readiness, rather than as an aspirational extension mentioned in a study's future-work section. Five of the seven studies in this corpus make exactly that kind of forward-looking claim (clinical adoption, integration into triage systems, supporting clinician decision-making) without the evaluation evidence to back it.

Fourth, and closing on the review's central contribution: the absence of emergency-medicine-specific regulatory guidance, layered on top of general AI/ML Software as a Medical Device frameworks that are still themselves maturing, means that governance considerations are, in this corpus, arriving only as a stated future-work item rather than as a constraint shaping evaluation design from the outset. This review's evidence does not establish that earlier governance engagement would have produced different findings. It establishes that the evidentiary gap regulatory frameworks are beginning to ask about is already present, at a scale (zero of seven studies meeting any of four criteria) that leaves very little room for the field to close it retroactively once deployment claims are already being made in the published literature.
