# Conclusion (draft v1)

**Status:** Draft narrative per `docs/manuscript/chapter_plan_v1.md` (~400 words target).

---

Seven studies applying post-hoc or inherently interpretable explanation methods to emergency department decision-support models met this review's eligibility criteria. Across all seven, computational evaluation of the explanation itself was common; evaluation involving a clinician was rare and, where present, informal or unvalidated; and evaluation meeting any of four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria drawn from current FDA and EU AI Act guidance was absent entirely. The single most rigorously validated study in the corpus by conventional model-quality standards was also one of the six with no human evaluation of its explanation at all, a pairing that recurs across this literature rather than being an isolated case.

This review's contribution is narrower than a general claim that the XAI field over-promises interpretability. It is that, specifically within emergency medicine, current evaluation practice does not yet produce evidence that would count as governance-compliant under the standards now emerging around clinical AI, and that bringing governance considerations into this work earlier, as a constraint on evaluation design from the outset rather than a compliance step applied after a system is already built, is both feasible and increasingly necessary. Explainability in emergency medicine should be held to a standard that is simultaneously standardized in what it reports, compliant with the evidentiary expectations now forming around it, and, ultimately, effective for the clinicians and patients it is meant to serve. At present, this review finds no included study that clears all three of those bars together, and most clear none.

This scoping review was conducted, and is reported here, as scaffolding rather than as an endpoint. Its findings define the evidentiary starting point, not the destination, for the empirical, clinician-in-the-loop evaluation work this literature currently lacks: studies that test whether an ED clinician's comprehension of an explanation is accurate, whether their reliance on a model tracks its actual reliability, and whether an explanation's presence changes a real disposition, triage, or intake decision for the better under the time and cognitive constraints that define emergency care. Until studies of that kind exist in this literature at meaningful scale, claims that emergency-medicine XAI systems are ready for clinical trust, let alone deployment, should be read as aspirations the evidence has not yet earned.
