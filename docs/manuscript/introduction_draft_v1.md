Emergency department (ED) clinicians are already being handed artificial-intelligence-generated explanations to support triage, acuity, and disposition decisions, under exactly the conditions least forgiving of a wrong call: severe time pressure, high cognitive load, and consequential, often irreversible outcomes. The premise behind deploying explainable AI (XAI) in this setting is straightforward: an explanation should help a clinician understand why a model produced a given output, calibrate how much to rely on it, and act on it appropriately. Whether that premise actually holds in practice, however, is an empirical question, and one this review finds has been tested remarkably rarely.

That untested assumption is colliding with a second, independent development. Regulatory frameworks are beginning to specify, in increasingly concrete terms, what evidence a clinical AI system's interpretability claims must be backed by before or alongside deployment. The FDA's guidance on AI/ML-based Software as a Medical Device and the EU AI Act's Article 13 transparency obligations both point toward a standard closer to demonstrated, evaluated interpretability than to an asserted or plausible one. The urgency this review takes as its starting point is therefore twofold: EM-specific XAI systems are already being proposed and, in some cases, described as deployment-ready, on the basis of evaluation evidence that, this review's own findings suggest, rarely reaches the clinician who would actually rely on it; and the regulatory expectations this evidence would need to satisfy are arriving now, not at some comfortable future point after the field has had time to mature.

This review organizes these concerns under three constructs. **Interpretability** is the degree to which an explanation supports clinician understanding and decision-making under the ED's specific time and cognitive constraints. **Trust calibration** is the alignment between a clinician's reliance on a model's output and that model's actual reliability, distinct from **explanation plausibility**, an explanation's capacity to seem convincing or satisfying independent of whether it is actually correct. **Evaluation validity** is the extent to which a study's design establishes a credible link between an explanation and the human decision behavior it is meant to inform. Together these define what this review calls interpretability validity: the extent to which current evaluation practice in emergency-medicine clinical XAI provides credible evidence that explanations are meaningful, appropriately trusted, and safe to use in real ED decision-making, rather than merely present.

## Where this review sits relative to existing literature

Three adjacent bodies of prior work bear on the questions this review asks, and none of them, individually or together, addresses the specific intersection this review targets.

The first is the general, technical XAI-methods literature: broad surveys of explanation methods, their mathematical properties, and their evaluation approaches, written without reference to any particular application domain (e.g., Adadi & Berrada, 2018; Vilone & Longo, 2021). These reviews are foundational to how this review itself classifies evaluation rigor (Section [Methods/Results, RQ2]), but they make no claim about clinical settings, let alone emergency medicine, at all.

The second is the broader clinical-XAI literature: reviews that examine explainable AI across hospital clinical decision support generally, spanning specialties from oncology to critical care to anaesthesiology (Van Dort et al., 2026). This body of work establishes that the plausibility-versus-calibration gap this review documents is not unique to emergency medicine; it is a recognized concern across hospital-based clinical decision support broadly. What it does not do is isolate the ED as a distinct decision-making context, one where the time pressure, the specific decision points (intake, acuity scoring, disposition), and the regulatory stakes are all different in kind from a radiology second-read or an oncology treatment-planning tool, and it does not map its findings against the specific regulatory-evidence criteria this review operationalizes.

The third is the emergency-medicine-specific AI and machine-learning literature: reviews of AI/ML triage and decision-support tools in the ED that are comprehensive about predictive performance, clinical outcomes, and implementation barriers, but for which explainability is, at most, a closing recommendation rather than the object of study (El Arab & Al Moosa, 2025). This is the closest existing work to this review's setting, and it is exactly where the gap is sharpest: a systematic review of AI in ED triage can note in passing that "explainable AI models foster trust and enable informed decisions" without that claim itself having been tested against the evidence, because testing it was never the review's purpose.

No existing review, to this review's knowledge, sits at the intersection of all three: emergency-medicine-specific scope, a framework distinguishing interpretability, trust calibration, and evaluation validity from one another, and an explicit mapping of the evidence this literature does or does not produce against the interpretability-validation expectations now emerging in regulatory guidance. This gap claim rests on the search described in the Methods section for prior reviews occupying this intersection, not on the size of this review's own included-study corpus, which is addressed separately below.

## Research questions

This review addresses three questions. **RQ1** asks which post-hoc explanation methods are deployed in EM clinical XAI studies and what forms of justification, coded inductively as computational, cognitive, or workflow-based, are provided for selecting them. **RQ2** asks how current studies evaluate explanation effectiveness across levels of human involvement, from computational metrics through clinician-in-the-loop and deployment-embedded evaluation, and what that distribution reveals about the rigor of current practice. **RQ3** asks what evaluation evidence current EM-focused XAI studies provide that would satisfy the interpretability-validation expectations emerging in regulatory frameworks, and where the most consequential evidentiary gaps lie.

Of these three, RQ2 supplies this review's evidentiary core: the charted distribution of evaluation approaches across the included studies is the empirical finding that makes RQ1 and RQ3 answerable rather than speculative. RQ3 supplies the review's contribution: mapping EM-specific evaluation practice against concrete, currently emerging regulatory expectations is, to this review's knowledge, a connection no prior review has made. RQ1 is necessary descriptive scaffolding for both: characterizing which methods are used and how their selection is justified sets up the terms in which RQ2's evaluation-rigor findings and RQ3's regulatory-evidence mapping can be interpreted.

This review is intended as scaffolding for the empirical, clinician-in-the-loop studies its own findings suggest are needed, not as a substitute for them.
