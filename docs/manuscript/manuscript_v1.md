## Abstract

**Background:** Explainable AI (XAI) systems are increasingly proposed for emergency department (ED) intake, triage, and disposition decisions, on the premise that explanations help clinicians understand and appropriately trust model outputs under severe time and cognitive constraints. Regulatory frameworks, including FDA AI/ML-based Software as a Medical Device guidance and EU AI Act Article 13, are beginning to specify what evidence such interpretability claims must be backed by. Whether current EM-specific XAI research produces that evidence has not previously been reviewed.

**Objective:** To characterize which post-hoc explanation methods are deployed in EM clinical XAI studies and how their selection is justified (RQ1); how these studies evaluate explanation effectiveness across levels of human involvement (RQ2); and what evidence they provide toward the interpretability-validation expectations emerging in regulatory guidance (RQ3).

**Methods:** A JBI-methodology scoping review, reported per PRISMA-ScR, searched PubMed, IEEE Xplore, and ACM Digital Library (2015 to search execution; Embase excluded for lack of institutional access). Of 650 deduplicated records, 64 were forwarded to full-text screening; 7 met eligibility criteria after 55 were assessed (9 unretrievable). Each was extracted against a 43-column schema mapping method, justification type, a six-level evaluation taxonomy, ecological validity, two independent evaluation-tier frameworks, and four regulatory-relevant-evidence indicators.

**Results:** Rule extraction, SHAP, and LIME each appeared in 2-3 of the 7 included studies; 43% gave no stated justification for method selection. Six of seven studies evaluated their explanation using computational proxy metrics alone, involving no human participant; none reached application-grounded evaluation. Zero of seven studies met any of the four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria assessed.

**Conclusions:** Current EM-specific XAI evaluation practice does not yet produce evidence that would satisfy emerging regulatory interpretability standards. This review is intended as scaffolding for the clinician-in-the-loop evaluation work this literature currently lacks.

**Keywords:** explainable artificial intelligence; emergency medicine; clinical decision support; interpretability; trust calibration; scoping review; regulatory evidence

## Introduction

Emergency department (ED) clinicians are already being handed artificial-intelligence-generated explanations to support triage, acuity, and disposition decisions, under exactly the conditions least forgiving of a wrong call: severe time pressure, high cognitive load, and consequential, often irreversible outcomes. The premise behind deploying explainable AI (XAI) in this setting is straightforward: an explanation should help a clinician understand why a model produced a given output, calibrate how much to rely on it, and act on it appropriately. Whether that premise actually holds in practice, however, is an empirical question, and one this review finds has been tested remarkably rarely.

That untested assumption is colliding with a second, independent development. Regulatory frameworks are beginning to specify, in increasingly concrete terms, what evidence a clinical AI system's interpretability claims must be backed by before or alongside deployment. The FDA's guidance on AI/ML-based Software as a Medical Device and the EU AI Act's Article 13 transparency obligations both point toward a standard closer to demonstrated, evaluated interpretability than to an asserted or plausible one. The urgency this review takes as its starting point is therefore twofold: EM-specific XAI systems are already being proposed and, in some cases, described as deployment-ready, on the basis of evaluation evidence that, this review's own findings suggest, rarely reaches the clinician who would actually rely on it; and the regulatory expectations this evidence would need to satisfy are arriving now, not at some comfortable future point after the field has had time to mature.

This review organizes these concerns under three constructs. **Interpretability** is the degree to which an explanation supports clinician understanding and decision-making under the ED's specific time and cognitive constraints. **Trust calibration** is the alignment between a clinician's reliance on a model's output and that model's actual reliability, distinct from **explanation plausibility**, an explanation's capacity to seem convincing or satisfying independent of whether it is actually correct. **Evaluation validity** is the extent to which a study's design establishes a credible link between an explanation and the human decision behavior it is meant to inform. Together these define what this review calls interpretability validity: the extent to which current evaluation practice in emergency-medicine clinical XAI provides credible evidence that explanations are meaningful, appropriately trusted, and safe to use in real ED decision-making, rather than merely present.

### Where this review sits relative to existing literature

Three adjacent bodies of prior work bear on the questions this review asks, and none of them, individually or together, addresses the specific intersection this review targets.

The first is the general, technical XAI-methods literature: broad surveys of explanation methods, their mathematical properties, and their evaluation approaches, written without reference to any particular application domain (e.g., Adadi & Berrada, 2018; Vilone & Longo, 2021). These reviews are foundational to how this review itself classifies evaluation rigor (RQ2, below), but they make no claim about clinical settings, let alone emergency medicine, at all.

The second is the broader clinical-XAI literature: reviews that examine explainable AI across hospital clinical decision support generally, spanning specialties from oncology to critical care to anaesthesiology (Van Dort et al., 2026). This body of work establishes that the plausibility-versus-calibration gap this review documents is not unique to emergency medicine; it is a recognized concern across hospital-based clinical decision support broadly. What it does not do is isolate the ED as a distinct decision-making context, one where the time pressure, the specific decision points (intake, acuity scoring, disposition), and the regulatory stakes are all different in kind from a radiology second-read or an oncology treatment-planning tool, and it does not map its findings against the specific regulatory-evidence criteria this review operationalizes.

The third is the emergency-medicine-specific AI and machine-learning literature: reviews of AI/ML triage and decision-support tools in the ED that are comprehensive about predictive performance, clinical outcomes, and implementation barriers, but for which explainability is, at most, a closing recommendation rather than the object of study (El Arab & Al Moosa, 2025). This is the closest existing work to this review's setting, and it is exactly where the gap is sharpest: a systematic review of AI in ED triage can note in passing that "explainable AI models foster trust and enable informed decisions" without that claim itself having been tested against the evidence, because testing it was never the review's purpose.

No existing review, to this review's knowledge, sits at the intersection of all three: emergency-medicine-specific scope, a framework distinguishing interpretability, trust calibration, and evaluation validity from one another, and an explicit mapping of the evidence this literature does or does not produce against the interpretability-validation expectations now emerging in regulatory guidance. This gap claim rests on the search described in the Methods section for prior reviews occupying this intersection, not on the size of this review's own included-study corpus, which is addressed separately below.

### Research questions

This review addresses three questions. **RQ1** asks which post-hoc explanation methods are deployed in EM clinical XAI studies and what forms of justification, coded inductively as computational, cognitive, or workflow-based, are provided for selecting them. **RQ2** asks how current studies evaluate explanation effectiveness across levels of human involvement, from computational metrics through clinician-in-the-loop and deployment-embedded evaluation, and what that distribution reveals about the rigor of current practice. **RQ3** asks what evaluation evidence current EM-focused XAI studies provide that would satisfy the interpretability-validation expectations emerging in regulatory frameworks, and where the most consequential evidentiary gaps lie.

Of these three, RQ2 supplies this review's evidentiary core: the charted distribution of evaluation approaches across the included studies is the empirical finding that makes RQ1 and RQ3 answerable rather than speculative. RQ3 supplies the review's contribution: mapping EM-specific evaluation practice against concrete, currently emerging regulatory expectations is, to this review's knowledge, a connection no prior review has made. RQ1 is necessary descriptive scaffolding for both: characterizing which methods are used and how their selection is justified sets up the terms in which RQ2's evaluation-rigor findings and RQ3's regulatory-evidence mapping can be interpreted.

This review is intended as scaffolding for the empirical, clinician-in-the-loop studies its own findings suggest are needed, not as a substitute for them.

## Methods

### Design

This review followed Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) scoping review methodology (Peters et al., 2020) and is reported per the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist (Tricco et al., 2018). Consistent with scoping review methodology generally, this review synthesizes findings narratively, organized by research question, rather than through meta-analysis or formal risk-of-bias-weighted pooling; the rationale for that choice is addressed under Synthesis below.

### Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they described an empirical application of a post-hoc or inherently interpretable explanation method to a machine-learning-based decision-support system targeting one of three emergency department (ED) decision points: patient intake, acuity or triage scoring, or immediate disposition. Full eligibility criteria, including the three-gate inclusion boundary (clinical domain and decision point; individual-patient-level decision; presence of clinician involvement in evaluation) and the associated exclusion taxonomy, are documented in the review's protocol materials. Studies were excluded if the decision point fell outside the three eligible categories (for example, inpatient or ICU settings, pre-hospital care, or diagnostic-differentiation tasks with no explicit link to an intake, triage, or disposition action); if no explanation method meeting the review's XAI taxonomy was present or evaluated; if the study was a review, protocol, or other non-primary-research format; or if insufficient methodological detail was reported to support extraction. Publication period was 2015-01-01 through search execution, in English.

### Information sources and search

Searches were executed across PubMed/MEDLINE, IEEE Xplore, and the ACM Digital Library (via the OpenAlex API, substituted for ACM's native search interface after the registered search string returned zero results even for single-term control queries). Embase was excluded from the primary search; the reviewer's institution does not subscribe, and no librarian-mediated search was available within the project timeline. This is reported as a disclosed limitation rather than a silently absorbed gap. The PubMed search (497 records) was executed 2026-06-10 using a three-concept string (explainability terms AND clinical/decision-support context AND ED-specific terms: triage, ESI, acuity, disposition), covering 2015-01-01 through the search-execution date. IEEE Xplore (161 records) and ACM Digital Library (1 record) were executed 2026-06-18 and 2026-06-22 respectively, using translations of the same three-concept string adapted to each database's syntax constraints.

### Study selection

Identification, screening, and eligibility counts are summarized in Figure 1 (PRISMA-ScR flow diagram). Searches identified 659 records (PubMed 497, IEEE Xplore 161, ACM 1). After removing 9 duplicates, 650 records proceeded to title-and-abstract screening; a structured retraction check against PubMed's metadata found none. A single reviewer screened all 650 records, yielding 40 bulk includes, 546 bulk excludes, and 64 borderline records requiring adjudication. On adjudication, 24 of the 64 borderline records were forwarded to full-text screening and 40 were excluded, bringing the total forwarded to full-text screening to 64 (40 bulk + 24 adjudicated).

Of the 64 records forwarded, 55 were assessed for eligibility; the remaining 9 could not be retrieved despite pursuing PMC's open-access API, direct publisher retrieval, and the reviewer's institutional access, and are reported as a distinct, disclosed category (not retrievable) rather than as excluded or silently dropped. Of the 55 assessed, 48 were excluded: 22 for an ineligible clinical domain or decision point (E1), 5 for no qualifying explanation component (E2), 13 for no empirical evaluation of the explanation method at any level (E3), and 8 for an ineligible publication format, principally reviews and protocols (E4). No record was excluded for insufficient extraction detail (E5) or as a duplicate report (E6). This yielded 7 included studies.

A post-hoc supplementary search of the gap window between the registered search's execution date and 2026-07-14, using the identical registered search string, was conducted after the main screening queue was exhausted, to check whether the included-study corpus was being under-sampled by publication lag. This supplement identified 46 additional candidate records, of which 6 were assessed at full-text (1 of 7 forwarded records was not retrievable) and none were included. The included-study corpus was finalized at 7 studies on this basis: continued search of a well-targeted, recent window returned zero additional eligible studies, indicating the corpus reflects the current state of the eligible literature rather than an artifact of the original search window's timing.

### Data extraction

Data were extracted using a 43-column schema developed iteratively across the review's exploratory and EM-specific phases. Columns map to this review's three research questions: RQ1 (method family, scope, and inductively coded justification type: computational, cognitive, workflow-based, or mixed); RQ2 (evaluation type across a six-level taxonomy from purely computational proxy metrics through downstream-outcome studies; a four-dimension ecological validity rubric scoring participant, task, environment, and outcome validity independently; and classification against two independent evaluation-tier frameworks, Doshi-Velez and Kim's (2017) functionally/human/application-grounded taxonomy and Vilone and Longo's (2021) objective-versus-human-centred, qualitative-versus-quantitative classification); and RQ3 (four boolean indicators of regulatory-relevant evidence: clinician comprehension, trust calibration, uncertainty and failure-mode transparency, and workflow safety, each requiring demonstrated rather than claimed evidence). A five-dimension quality and risk-of-bias rubric, adapted from QUADAS-2, RoB 2, and TRIPOD, was applied independently of the ecological validity dimensions, since methodological execution quality and ecological realism are conceptually distinct properties of a study.

### Synthesis

Findings are synthesized narratively, organized by research question, following JBI scoping review convention. Meta-analysis was not conducted; it is not an appropriate method for a heterogeneous set of studies varying in ED decision point, explanation method, and evaluation design, and scoping review methodology does not require it. Findings are reported and interpreted at the scale of the included corpus (seven studies meeting this review's specific eligibility criteria), not generalized to emergency-medicine clinical XAI as a field.

### Limitations acknowledged in the design

Two features of this review's design are stated directly here rather than deferred to a closing limitations section. First, extraction and screening were conducted by a single reviewer; this is an appropriate scope for the project this review was conducted under and is not treated as a methodological gap requiring mitigation, though it is disclosed as a departure from dual-reviewer convention. Second, the small size of the final included-study corpus (7 of 650 initially identified records) is reported as a substantive finding about the state of this specific literature, not apologized for: it reflects the outcome of an exhaustive, registered search and screening process, corroborated by the supplementary search finding zero additional eligible studies in a recent, well-targeted window, rather than an arbitrary or premature stopping point.

## Results

Seven studies met this review's eligibility criteria for inclusion, all published between 2021 and 2026, each applying a post-hoc explanation method to a machine learning model trained on structured or free-text ED data.

### Methods and Justification (RQ1)

Three explanation-method families appeared across the seven included studies: rule extraction (n=3), SHAP (n=2), and LIME (n=2). No study in this corpus applied a counterfactual, example-based, or attention-based method, and no study compared more than one explanation-method family within the same evaluation. Two of the three rule-extraction studies (Sulaiman et al., 2025; Sulaiman et al., 2023) used TE2Rules to extract a global rule list as a post-hoc surrogate of a separately trained gradient-boosting model; the third (Xie et al., 2021) instead produced an inherently interpretable additive point-based score (SERP, via the AutoScore framework), where the explanation is structurally identical to the deployed model rather than an approximation of it.

Justification for method selection was inconsistently reported. Three studies (43%) gave no stated rationale for choosing their explanation method over an alternative; three (43%) combined more than one type of justification; and one (14%) gave a purely computational rationale. Critically, no study in this corpus offered a cognitive-only or workflow-only justification in isolation, and no study explicitly compared its chosen method against a named alternative. Where a rationale was given, it most often invoked a method's theoretical or computational properties (model-agnosticism, Shapley-value consistency) rather than an empirical claim about clinician comprehension or workflow fit. Mapped onto the review's three eligible ED decision points, four studies targeted disposition-related outcomes and three targeted acuity or triage-level risk stratification; none targeted the initial intake decision point specifically.

### Evaluation Rigor (RQ2)

The distribution of evaluation approaches in this corpus is concentrated at the least human-involved end of the spectrum. Five of seven studies (71%) evaluated their explanation using computational proxy metrics only, with no human participant at any stage. One study (Arnaud et al., 2023) involved a single physician co-author who informally reviewed two illustrative explanation examples, an evaluation activity too limited to meet this review's minimum threshold for any named evaluation type. One study (Juang et al., 2026) administered an unvalidated two-question survey on clinical utility and trustworthiness, the only evaluation activity in the corpus that involved an assigned task completed by independent clinical participants.

This distribution is reflected consistently across the review's two evaluation-tier frameworks. Under the Doshi-Velez and Kim (2017) framework, six of seven studies (86%) are functionally grounded, and one (14%) is human grounded; no study reaches application-grounded evaluation, the tier corresponding to real end-users completing the real target task in a realistic setting. Under the independent Vilone and Longo (2021) classification, five studies (71%) are purely objective/automated, and two (29%) are human-centred but qualitative in nature; no study reaches a human-centred quantitative or mixed evaluation. The ecological validity profile is similarly concentrated: six of seven studies score zero across all four ecological-validity dimensions, and the sole exception, Juang et al. (2026), reaches a composite score of only 6 out of a possible 12. No study in this corpus reaches Realism_Level ≥ 3 (ecologically representative evaluation) or higher.

Notably, evaluation rigor did not track the technical rigor of the underlying prediction model. The single largest and most thoroughly validated study in the corpus (Xie et al., 2021; n = 323,509 admissions, national death-registry-linked outcome, external temporal validation, scoring the maximum on all five quality and risk-of-bias dimensions extracted) was evaluated for interpretability using proxy metrics alone, with no clinician or lay evaluator involved in assessing whether its explanation was comprehensible, trustworthy, or usable.

### Regulatory Readiness (RQ3)

None of the seven included studies provided evidence meeting any of the four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria operationalized from FDA AI/ML-based Software as a Medical Device guidance and EU AI Act Article 13. All four criteria were coded absent across the entire corpus (0/7, 0% for each criterion).

No study administered a comprehension check or a correct-interpretation task to any clinician. On trust calibration, every trust-related claim in the corpus fell into one of two categories: no claim at all (4 of 7 studies) or an assertion of explanation plausibility unaccompanied by any test of whether clinician trust actually tracked model reliability (3 of 7 studies); no study reached this review's calibrated code. On uncertainty and failure-mode transparency, several studies reported confidence intervals or discussed model-confidence limitations in their own Discussion sections, but in every case this information was a research-reporting statistic directed at other researchers rather than a feature surfaced to a clinician at the point of use. On workflow safety, no study evaluated its explanation within a realistic clinical workflow, and the one study with a deployed interface (Han et al., 2025) demonstrated it only through author-run illustrative cases rather than clinician use.

## Discussion

Across the seven studies meeting this review's eligibility criteria, a consistent pattern emerges that cuts across method family, publication venue, and underlying model quality: an explanation is built, and in most cases a computational property of that explanation is measured, but almost nothing is measured about whether the explanation actually helps, or could safely be trusted by, the clinician it is nominally for. Six of seven studies never involve a human at any stage of evaluating the explanation. The one exception offers an unvalidated two-question survey. Not one study reaches this review's application-grounded evaluation tier, and not one satisfies any of the four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria drawn from current FDA and EU AI Act guidance. Framed against this review's three research questions, RQ2's evaluation-rigor findings are the evidentiary core of this result, and RQ3's regulatory mapping is where that evidentiary gap becomes consequential rather than merely descriptive: it is the corpus's near-uniform absence of human-grounded and application-grounded evaluation (RQ2) that directly produces its near-uniform failure to meet any regulatory-relevant-evidence criterion (RQ3). RQ1's method-and-justification findings supply necessary scaffolding for both, showing that the methods deployed are rarely chosen for a stated, comparative reason in the first place.

### Dialogue with prior work

The gap this review documents is not a new concern in the broader XAI literature. Automation-trust research has long distinguished an operator's confidence in a system from whether that confidence actually tracks the system's real reliability, a distinction formalized as appropriate reliance and elaborated across dimensions of trust calibration well before XAI-specific evaluation frameworks existed (Lee & See, 2004; Dzindolet et al., 2003). Doshi-Velez and Kim (2017) later proposed the functionally-grounded, human-grounded, and application-grounded tiers this review adopts for RQ2, explicitly to distinguish evaluations that involve no human, a human on a simplified task, and a human on the real task; and the evaluation-approaches literature separately distinguishes objective, automated assessment from human-centred assessment as two different things a study can do (Vilone & Longo, 2021). What this review adds is not any of these distinctions in themselves but evidence that, within emergency-medicine-specific applications of post-hoc explanation methods, the human-centred, calibration-oriented half of each of these distinctions is essentially unaddressed.

A reader might reasonably ask whether this is simply what a young, methods-focused subfield looks like, and whether it is unfair to hold seven studies to a regulatory-evidence standard that most of clinical AI has not yet met either. Two responses follow from the data itself rather than from a general defense of the field. First, the corpus's single most rigorously validated study by the extraction's own quality criteria (Xie et al., 2021, scoring the maximum on all five extracted quality dimensions, validated externally on tens of thousands of admissions) is also one of the six studies with zero evaluation of its explanation's effect on any human. Methodological maturity in model validation and methodological maturity in interpretability validation are demonstrably separable in this corpus, not two faces of the same underlying rigor. Second, the claim this review makes is descriptive, not causal: the evidence gap documented here is not evidence that earlier regulatory engagement would have closed it, only that the gap exists at a moment when regulatory frameworks are beginning to ask for exactly this kind of evidence pre-deployment, not after. That regulatory clock is already running independent of whether the field considers itself ready for it.

### What this corpus is small enough, and specific enough, to say

Seven included studies, drawn from an initial pool of 650 title-and-abstract records, is not a limitation to apologize for so much as a finding in its own right: applying an emergency-medicine-specific inclusion boundary to the growing clinical-XAI literature returns very few studies with any human evaluation component at all, which is itself evidence of how early-stage this specific intersection is. What follows should be read at that scale. This review does not claim that emergency-medicine XAI as a field fails to evaluate explanations; it claims that, among the seven studies that met this review's inclusion criteria, none did so beyond a functionally-grounded or, in one case, an unvalidated human-grounded level, and specifies exactly which of four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria each one failed to meet and why.

### Recommendations

Three recommendations follow directly from patterns in the extracted data, and a fourth follows from what the data as a whole implies about when governance considerations should enter this work.

First, a reporting-standard proposal: future emergency-medicine XAI studies should report explicitly against the four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria operationalized in this review's extraction schema, in the way that existing AI-specific reporting guidelines already require explicit reporting of specific methodological elements at different stages of the clinical AI pipeline: TRIPOD+AI for prediction-model development and validation (Collins et al., 2024), CONSORT-AI for randomized trials of AI interventions (Liu et al., 2020), and DECIDE-AI specifically for early-stage clinical evaluation of AI-driven decision support systems (Vasey et al., 2022), the pipeline stage most directly analogous to what this review's included studies attempt and mostly fall short of.

Second, a design recommendation grounded directly in the method-interface isolation extraction: only one of the seven studies (Juang et al., 2026) presents its explanation through any human-facing interface at all, and even that study does not isolate the explanation method's effect from the interface's presentation choices, since only one method and one interface configuration were tested. Future work that does reach a human-centred evaluation should build in this isolation explicitly, comparing either multiple explanation methods through an identical interface or a fixed method across interface variants, so that an observed effect on comprehension or trust can be attributed to the explanation method itself rather than confounded with how it happened to be displayed.

Third, an evaluation-tier push: given that six of seven studies in this corpus never move past a functionally-grounded, proxy-metric evaluation, the field would benefit from treating application-grounded evaluation, real clinicians completing the real target decision in a realistic setting, as a precondition for any public claim about clinical trust, decision support, or deployment readiness, rather than as an aspirational extension mentioned in a study's future-work section. Five of the seven studies in this corpus make exactly that kind of forward-looking claim without the evaluation evidence to back it.

Fourth, and closing on the review's central contribution: the absence of emergency-medicine-specific regulatory guidance, layered on top of general AI/ML Software as a Medical Device frameworks that are still themselves maturing, means that governance considerations are, in this corpus, arriving only as a stated future-work item rather than as a constraint shaping evaluation design from the outset. This review's evidence does not establish that earlier governance engagement would have produced different findings. It establishes that the evidentiary gap regulatory frameworks are beginning to ask about is already present, at a scale that leaves very little room for the field to close it retroactively once deployment claims are already being made in the published literature.

## Conclusion

Seven studies applying post-hoc or inherently interpretable explanation methods to emergency department decision-support models met this review's eligibility criteria. Across all seven, computational evaluation of the explanation itself was common; evaluation involving a clinician was rare and, where present, informal or unvalidated; and evaluation meeting any of four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria drawn from current FDA and EU AI Act guidance was absent entirely. The single most rigorously validated study in the corpus by conventional model-quality standards was also one of the six with no human evaluation of its explanation at all, a pairing that recurs across this literature rather than being an isolated case.

This review's contribution is narrower than a general claim that the XAI field over-promises interpretability. It is that, specifically within emergency medicine, current evaluation practice does not yet produce evidence that would count as governance-compliant under the standards now emerging around clinical AI, and that bringing governance considerations into this work earlier, as a constraint on evaluation design from the outset rather than a compliance step applied after a system is already built, is both feasible and increasingly necessary. Explainability in emergency medicine should be held to a standard that is simultaneously standardized in what it reports, compliant with the evidentiary expectations now forming around it, and, ultimately, effective for the clinicians and patients it is meant to serve. At present, this review finds no included study that clears all three of those bars together, and most clear none.

This scoping review was conducted, and is reported here, as scaffolding rather than as an endpoint. Its findings define the evidentiary starting point, not the destination, for the empirical, clinician-in-the-loop evaluation work this literature currently lacks: studies that test whether an ED clinician's comprehension of an explanation is accurate, whether their reliance on a model tracks its actual reliability, and whether an explanation's presence changes a real disposition, triage, or intake decision for the better under the time and cognitive constraints that define emergency care. Until studies of that kind exist in this literature at meaningful scale, claims that emergency-medicine XAI systems are ready for clinical trust, let alone deployment, should be read as aspirations the evidence has not yet earned.
