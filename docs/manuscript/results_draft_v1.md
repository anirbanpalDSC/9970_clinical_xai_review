# Results (draft v1)

Seven studies met this review's eligibility criteria for inclusion (see Methods; full-text screening closed at 7 included studies of 55 assessed, 2026-07-14). All seven were published between 2021 and 2026 and applied a post-hoc explanation method to a machine learning model trained on structured or free-text emergency department (ED) data. Table 1, Table 2, and Table 3 report the charted extraction results for RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3 respectively; full per-study extraction values and coding rationale are available in `data/extraction/schema_v1.csv` and `data/extraction/per_paper/`.

## Methods and Justification (RQ1)

Three explanation-method families appeared across the seven included studies: rule extraction (n=3), SHAP (n=2), and LIME (n=2). No study in this corpus applied a counterfactual, example-based, or attention-based method, and no study compared more than one explanation-method family within the same evaluation. Two of the three rule-extraction studies (Sulaiman et al., 2025; Sulaiman et al., 2023) used TE2Rules to extract a global rule list as a post-hoc surrogate of a separately trained gradient-boosting model; the third (Xie et al., 2021) instead produced an inherently interpretable additive point-based score (SERP, via the AutoScore framework), where the explanation is structurally identical to the deployed model rather than an approximation of it.

Justification for method selection was inconsistently reported. Three studies (43%) gave no stated rationale for choosing their explanation method over an alternative (`Method_Justification_Type = NotReported`); three (43%) combined more than one type of justification (`Mixed`); and one (14%) gave a purely computational rationale. Critically, no study in this corpus offered a cognitive-only or workflow-only justification in isolation, and no study explicitly compared its chosen method against a named alternative (for example, stating why SHAP was selected over LIME, or vice versa). Where a rationale was given, it most often invoked a method's theoretical or computational properties (model-agnosticism, Shapley-value consistency) rather than an empirical claim about clinician comprehension or workflow fit. Mapped onto the review's three eligible ED decision points, four studies targeted disposition-related outcomes (hospitalization, admission, revisit-driven discharge planning, or serious disposition after a fall) and three targeted acuity or triage-level risk stratification; none targeted the initial intake decision point specifically.

## Evaluation Rigor (RQ2)

The distribution of evaluation approaches in this corpus is concentrated at the least human-involved end of the spectrum. Five of seven studies (71%) evaluated their explanation using computational proxy metrics only (`Eval_Type = ProxyMetric`): feature-importance stability, rule fidelity, or coverage statistics computed with no human participant at any stage. One study (`Arnaud et al., 2023`) involved a single physician co-author who informally reviewed two illustrative explanation examples, an evaluation activity too limited to meet this review's minimum threshold for any named evaluation type (`Eval_Type = None`). One study (`Juang et al., 2026`) administered an unvalidated two-question survey on clinical utility and trustworthiness to an unspecified number of physicians, the only evaluation activity in the corpus that involved an assigned task completed by independent clinical participants (`Eval_Type = TrustQuestionnaire`).

This distribution is reflected consistently across the review's two evaluation-tier frameworks. Under the Doshi-Velez and Kim (2017) framework, six of seven studies (86%) are functionally grounded, and one (14%) is human grounded; no study reaches application-grounded evaluation, the tier corresponding to real end-users completing the real target task in a realistic setting. Under the independent Vilone and Longo (2021) classification, five studies (71%) are purely objective/automated, and two (29%) are human-centred but qualitative in nature; no study in the corpus reaches a human-centred quantitative or mixed evaluation. The ecological validity profile is similarly concentrated: six of seven studies score zero across all four ecological-validity dimensions (participant, task, environment, and outcome validity), and the sole exception, `Juang et al. (2026)`, reaches a composite score of only 6 out of a possible 12, driven by real clinicians completing an artificial survey task through a bespoke research interface rather than an assigned clinical decision in a realistic setting. No study in this corpus reaches `Realism_Level >= 3` (ecologically representative evaluation) or higher.

Notably, evaluation rigor as operationalized by this review's five-level taxonomy did not track the technical rigor of the underlying prediction model. The single largest and most thoroughly validated study in the corpus (`Xie et al., 2021`; n = 323,509 admissions, national death-registry-linked outcome, external temporal validation, scoring the maximum on all five quality and risk-of-bias dimensions extracted) was evaluated for interpretability using proxy metrics alone, with no clinician or lay evaluator involved in assessing whether its explanation was comprehensible, trustworthy, or usable.

## Regulatory Readiness (RQ3)

None of the seven included studies provided evidence meeting any of the four regulatory-relevant-evidence criteria operationalized from FDA AI/ML-based Software as a Medical Device guidance and EU AI Act Article 13 (clinician comprehension, trust calibration, uncertainty/failure-mode transparency, and workflow safety). All four criteria were coded `No` across the entire corpus (0/7, 0% for each criterion).

No study administered a comprehension check or a correct-interpretation task to any clinician; the closest cases involved a clinician viewing or informally commenting on an explanation output, never being tested on whether that explanation was correctly understood. On trust calibration, every trust-related claim in the corpus fell into one of two categories: no claim at all (`Trust_Claim = None`, 4 of 7 studies) or an assertion of explanation plausibility unaccompanied by any test of whether clinician trust actually tracked model reliability (`Trust_Claim = Plausibility`, 3 of 7 studies). No study reached this review's `Calibrated` code. On uncertainty and failure-mode transparency, several studies reported confidence intervals or discussed model-confidence limitations in their own Discussion sections, but in every case this information was a research-reporting statistic directed at other researchers rather than a feature surfaced to a clinician at the point of use. On workflow safety, the pattern was consistent with the ecological-validity findings above: no study evaluated its explanation within a realistic clinical workflow, and the one study with a deployed interface (`Han et al., 2025`) demonstrated it only through author-run illustrative cases rather than clinician use.
